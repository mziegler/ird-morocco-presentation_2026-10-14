Technology Inequities

But we know that the spread of computing technology hasn't been fair to everyone.

For example, internet access and device ownership haven't been fairly distributed. This map shows how broadband internet is much more easily available in some parts of the world than others, for example.

This sort of problem is really well-known to folks here at MSRI, who have done some of the most pioneering work on ICT4D.

AI Model Biases

Credit: Joy Buolamwini, Reuters/Richard Lee
Another well-established problem is discriminatory biases in AI models.

The example on this book cover on the left illustrates how racism and misogyny are embedded in AI language models,

We see similar problems in visual models. For example in one famous case, the passport application website for New Zealand was rejecting photos of East Asian people because it thought their eyes were closed.

In this other example, researchers measured that Amazon's facial recognition product guessed the genders less accurately for women of darker skin tones, tending to erroniously label them as men.

AI model biases are starting to have more real-world impacts on people as they find their way into real products and applications that we interact with. For example, there have been cases of wrongful arrests because of errors in facial recognition.

Environmental Inequities: Climate


*Adapted from Our World in Data
Climate change is one of the most important issues affecting the ocean. Many of you have probably seen maps like this, showing greenhouse gas emissions per capita. We see that most of the warming emissions come from people in the "developed world," historically affluent countries.

However, climate change effects everyone on earth; and it is more damaging in regions close to the equator that are already hot.

The rich countries had their opportunity to develop largely from fossil fuels, and now we are asking the developing countries to stop developing, for this problem that was not really caused by them, but was actually caused by the rich countries. This is one example of inequity.

Gendered Fisheries Labor

Photo credits: Asian Development Bank; Peninsula Daily News; Alaskan Seafood Marketing Institute; RARE
Another example of how equity comes up in ocean governence is gendered labor.

In many places around the world, fisheries labor is gendered, like men more often go onto deep-sea boats, and women handle the business and processing and near-shore fishing. This varies from place to place.

But these gender roles make it so fisheries management decisions affect men and women differntly, so we need to think about gender equity. For example, policies that priotitize deep-sea or industrial fishing can be more harmful to women, if they lose access to markets or to near-shore fisheries.

So, here are some important questions to consider for designing equitable ocean technology. We are formulating those draft guidelines starting with these questions.

1. In an ocean technology project, who has decision-making power?

2. Who is excluded? Either by technology access barriers, or by design factors.

3. Who benefits from model biases? We are starting to see a lot more AI applications in ocean governance, so this is starting to become really important.

4. Who takes on burdens and risks? In an environmental technology project, these can be anything from extra work for some stakeholders, expenses, security and privacy risks, or reorganizing their institutions around the technology.

5. Who benefits from hype? What do I mean by this?

Technology is not the solution to every environmental problem, but new technologies tend to bring a lot of hype, excitement, and confusion; and the hype has the effect of greenwashing, distracting resources away from other issues, or helping avoid regulation.

Importantly, I'm not saying that ocean technology is bad or that we shouldn't do it.
- In fact, I think it's really important.
- I'm saying that these are some conversations that we need to start having, in order to do it right.

- And this list is not exhaustive.

For the rest of this presentation, I'm going to go through each of these questions and give details and examples.

We also are right on the cusp of computer vision becoming much more widespread in ocean management, very soon. There are lots of different applications in early-stage development that seem to be getting good results, and governments and NGO's are getting ready to implement them at scale.

For example, there are projects using computer vision
- to identify and count fish underwater as they go into a net
- to monitor onboard fishing boats, identifying and counting all the fish that get caught or thrown overboard, often also predicting their sex and age,
- and also lots of different applications using computer vision with sattelite images, like trying to identify the locations of fishing boats.

The bias issues with these vision models are a little different from language models, but still related.
- they will make errors sometimes, and the errors won't always be evenly distributed, possibly behaving unfairly to some people. I want to be cautious about these potential biases when computer vision is used for controversial management decisions, or for enforcement.

- A lot of these applications will be using foundation models fine-tuned for specific applications. Like there might be a foundation model for video enfocement on fishing boats, for exaple, and the model would be fine-tuned for a certain type of boat or a certain region. I think we need to start thinking about the governance of these foundation models so that they are fair for everyone.

- since these uses of computer vision are all so new, we will likely need a lot of research into their equity, fairness, and accountability.

-- This is a direction that we will be working on in the future; this will become really important soon as we see more and more computer vision in ocean governence.

To talk about decision-making power, let's go into the example of modeling for fisheries management processes.

Really complex and computationally-intensive models are becoming more and more common in different oceanic menagement processes. This slide has a couple diagrams just to show how complex they are, with all these systems of differential equations.

More and more of these models require a lot of computational power. It's common for a model to take something like 2 weeks to run, on heavy expensive cloud computing infrastructure.

So what is the equity issue with modeling?

These ocean management models tend to get used in highly political and contested negotiation processes.

For example, Tuna
--- model tuna populations in different places under different management strategies
--- decide what quotas to set for each country,
--- where to put no-fishing zones, things like that. These decisions have significant economic consequences for many countries.

- And the ever-increasing complexity of these models makes it harder for smaller, less-powerful stakeholders to participate.

This is important because the models take a certain point of view: what is modeled and why? What assumptions does the model make? Some things are prioritized, other things left out.
- These choices can be really political.
- When the models become so complicated, it has the effect of squeezing out less powerful stakeholders who have less technical capacity for modeling.

For some more depth, I'd like to refer you to Angela Abolhassani's work, an Ocean Nexus fellow, on how management procedures built on modeling frameworks can obscure these power imbalances.

There are a variety of "participatory modeling processes" that try to bridge this gap, but in practice they often fall short because the more powerful actors still keep control of the whole modelling process.

So, what do we do about this?

- There are some ways we can partially address this problem with technology changes, like using simpler models to lower the barrier to participation, or by building better user interfaces with more interactivity.

So now let's move to our next consideration for designing equitable ocean technologies: Who is excluded?

There are a few different ways that relying on technology can exclude some people from an initiative.

Example: Mobile Apps for Fishers

  • PescaData: mobile app for small fishing cooperatives
  • Exploring new features: AI knowledge base? AI assistant?
  • Technology access barriers:
    • Mixed formal education levels
    • Literacy barriers
    • Generational differences
    • Gender differences
    • Online social exclusion
    • Bad connectivity at sea
      Claudia Delgado Ramírez et. al., forthcoming
  • Could AI help, or worsen inclusivity?
    • User-friendly conversation could bridge access barriers
    • AI literacy gap: potential for misplaced trust

To talk about one of them, we'll use the example of PescaData, a mobile app for fishing cooperatives to manage data to get environmental certifications, among other features like connecting to other fishers.
- There are many other ishing mobile app projects, so this gives us a case study.

A key goal of PescaData is to provide services to small and underserved fishing cooperatives.

However somewhat paradoxically, serving the most underserved fishing cooperatives is hard to do with a mobile app because these most-marginalized fishers often have the biggest barriers for using technology.

For example, many of the fishers had difficulties using the app, stemming from
- mixed formal education levels
- and literacy barriers.
- These barriers to using the app tended to fall along generational lines,
- as well as gender.
- Additionally, a access challenge for these fishing mobile app projects is that there just is not good cellular connectivity at sea.

More details on this work will be available soon in a forthcoming publication that I am writing with anthropoligist Claudia Delgado Ramírez.

So, what can you do about these technology access barriers?
- One is to center the most marginalized users in your design process. When you design specifically for the people who will have the most trouble with your app, then your other users tend to also benefit.

- Another is to use diverse communication channels; expect that some people will want to use your app and others won't, so you can use a multi-pronged strategy to meet people where they are.

- In some circumstances, it can help to invest in training for an app.

Let's move on to our next consideration: who takes on burdens and risks?

These last couple ones are quick.

By burdens, I mean that ocean technology initiatives can create extra work for some stakeholders, expenses, security and privacy risks, or reorganizing their institutions around the technology, among other things.

Who takes on burdens and risks?

Example: Fishing Boat Surveillance

  • Monitoring systems including AIS, VMS, camera systems
  • Need to balance fishers' privacy and security with enforcement needs
  • Partial technical mitigations: blurring faces with AI
To talk about burdens, we have this example of fishing boat surveillance.

There are several new forms of electronic monitoring that are in development for fishing vessels, like various systems tracking boats with sattelites, and camera systems. More and more governments and buyers are starting to require that fishing boats have cameras installed in certain places.

And lots of fishers are pretty unhappy about this. This slide has some example images from these video monitoring systems, and to me this feels very invasive. It would be like having a video camera in my office all day. To me, it seems completely reasonable that fishers don't like this.

I don't know what the solution is, but we need to take fisher's privacy concerns seriously instead of downplaying them. This is just an example of how we need to think about who takes on the burdens in these technology projects.

Again, there are some technical approaches to partially mitigate this problem, like trying to blur faces with AI. This is not a complete solution though.

Now we're at our last topic: who benefits from hype?

I am still trying to figure out how to talk about this one in the context of equity. I think it's really important but it's also delicate.

New technology tends to generate a lot of excitement and confusion; and it has the effect of shifting around power and resources. Technology hype can cause some issues to receive more attention than others, or take resources away from other kinds of work. Some powerful actors use it to their advantage for greenwashing, appearing more environmental than they actually are, or helping powerful actors avoid regulation.

This happens often enough that I think it's a real problem for environmental governance.

Who benefits from technology hype?

Example: Blockchain Traceability

Here is one more example of this problem: there has been a lot of hype about blockchain being used for traceability in the fish supply chain.
- The ideas is that: by using blockchain to trace a fish through all of the transactions from the fisher to the consumer, we can stop illegally-caught fish from entering the supply chain and being sold as legally-caught fish.

You can find hundreds of press releases and headlines about companies claiming to "bring unprecedented transparency to their supply chain" using blockchain, and stopping illegal fishing, et cetera. I went to the World Fisheries Congress back in March, and everybody had heard of blockchain but almost nobody actually understood what it is.

FRAGMENT

However, we are several years into this, and the impact on fisheries has been minimal for several reasons including technical implementation problems and corporate politics, and there are still many other ways to commit fraud in the supply chain.



There is a real danger here that the hype around this technology can create greenwashing and a false appearance of accountability; making it look like companies are behaving more sustainably than they actually are. This can have the effect of helping bad actors avoid regulation, potentially making the problem worse.

Who benefits from technology hype?

Photo credits: Ran Marine; The Coca Cola Company
So, here are a couple examples. You might have heard of these projects building robots to clean up the plastic in the ocean. These have received a lot of attention, investment, and media coverage.

The intention is good, but it remains to be seen whether these ocean-cleanup robots will be enough to make a meaningful difference to the problem. So far, their impact has been negligable.

FRAGMENT

Meanwile, plastic production is still accelerating. Last year we produced more plastic than any year before. Over 90% of plastic goes into single-use products, like packaging. This is the root problem that we need to be addressing, not just cleaning it up once it is already in the ocean.

Reducing plastic production is really difficult though, and a solution seems far away. Politicians don't want to do it because of industry pressures.

There is a real danger that the hype about ocean cleanup robots is taking the pressure off of us to reduce plastic production. So we think we will have a technical solution with robots to clean it up for us, so we don't have to do the hard thing.

There is a danger that technology hype gives power to some powerful stakeholders and helps them avoid regulation; and makes it harder for other solutions to get attention.
